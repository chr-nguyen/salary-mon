# Salary-mon: cuteness, charm, and a cozy visual identity

Research and implementation brief · 14 October 2026

## Recommendation

Make Salary-mon a **small, inhabited storybook room beside your working day**. Use soft, distinct creatures; warm material detail; a quiet paper interface; and characterful responses. Keep the creature-raising and collecting heritage of the retro concept, but let visible pixels become an optional future skin rather than the main source of personality.

The selected Room diary concept is a better foundation for this audience than a dense handheld-console dashboard. A central room can carry both affection and future collection progress. The timer remains easy to read and operate, but the creature is the emotional lead.

This is a design recommendation, not a claim that a single visual style represents all cozy gamers or Japanese players. Cozy is also a property of pacing, agency, and consequences. An attractive room cannot compensate for punishing mechanics.

## What the evidence actually supports

### 1. Infant-like proportions can invite nurturing

Glocker et al. (2009) manipulated photographs of infant faces and asked 122 undergraduates to rate cuteness and caretaking motivation. Higher baby-schema faces received higher ratings. The experimentally tested features included rounder faces and higher foreheads. This is evidence about human infant photographs and reported motivation, not an experimentally established formula for successful game mascots. [Paper and abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1439-0310.2008.01603.x).

**Application, as art direction:** give a companion a generous upper face, small lower facial features, a compact body, and short limbs. Simplify enough that the expression remains legible on a phone. Large anime eyes are optional: our small dark eyes sit in an ample, rounded face. Do not maximize every baby-like feature until the character becomes generic or infantile.

### 2. Rounded contours are a useful starting point, not a universal preference law

Bar and Neta (2006) investigated preferences for curved versus sharp-angled visual objects. Their work supports a preference for curvature in the tested stimuli. It does not show that every corner should be round or that curves guarantee commercial appeal. [Research publication](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2006.01759.x/).

**Application:** use broad, soft silhouettes and small blunt appendages. Preserve some contrast in the design: Sumi's ink-drop top and pencil distinguish it from Haku's cloud outline. The interface uses round cards and buttons, while text and timer numerals remain crisp. Eliminating all shape contrast would make the cast harder to identify.

### 3. Cuteness is not the same thing as coziness

The 2017 Project Horseshoe designer report frames cozy play around safety, abundance, and softness, and distinguishes it from cuteness alone. It discusses warm, softened light, familiar objects, low-demand companionship, and voluntary interactions. It also identifies intrusive feedback and compulsory maintenance as possible sources of pressure. This is a practitioner framework, not a controlled experiment or current market-sizing study. [Original report](https://www.projecthorseshoe.com/reports/featured/ph17r3.htm).

**Application:** a room with a place to sit, a blanket, a cup, and a garden outside conveys a settled life. A companion that responds when invited feels different from one with a constantly draining hunger meter. Work timers should count and record rather than threaten failure. High saturation and alert-like flashes belong only to genuine information that needs attention.

### 4. Cute imagery can affect attention, but don't sell it as a productivity treatment

Nittono et al. (2012) ran three experiments involving cute images and subsequent focused tasks. They reported more careful task performance and narrowed attentional focus in those laboratory contexts. The samples were university students, and the stimuli and tasks were not a long-term work-tracking game. These findings do not establish that Salary-mon improves productivity, wellbeing, or retention. [Open-access study](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0046362).

**Application:** put expressive reactions at a natural stopping point. Keep active-work visuals quiet. Give people a companion to enjoy without making an efficacy claim.

### 5. Familiar objects can make a place emotionally specific

Unpacking's official description connects domestic objects, arranging rooms, and discovering a character's story; its stated experience has no timers or scores. Salary-mon necessarily has a work timer, but it can borrow the role of objects as memories rather than as anonymous loot. [Official game site](https://www.unpackinggame.com/).

Cozy Grove's developer describes hand-drawn landscapes that respond when players help spirits. That gives us a useful example of the environment making an action's result visible. It is not evidence that hand-drawn art itself causes retention. [Spry Fox's game description](https://spryfox.com/our-games/cozy-grove/).

**Application:** a found leaf, a mismatched cup, or a souvenir illustration should eventually appear in the room. The same object can recur in a greeting or diary entry. The art needs a clear quiet space for these additions; filling every corner at launch would leave no room for player authorship.

### 6. Japanese character references suggest specificity and vulnerability, not one national style

San-X's official Sumikko Gurashi materials pair small character identities with comfort in corners and an affectionate, gently odd tone. The useful lesson is that a minor preference or vulnerability can be a character's premise. This is a brand reference, not proof of Japanese consumers' general preferences. [Official character page](https://www.san-x.co.jp/ja/characters/sumikkogurashi/), [San-X's character description in its release](https://www.san-x.co.jp/release/25.06-18_RUNDONE_SG.pdf).

**Application:** give each Salary-mon one memorable habit. Avoid defaulting to “pink + blush + baby talk.” Keep creature names short and friendly, and keep important controls in natural, readable Japanese. A reserved greeting or a tiny offered seat can be more distinctive than constant hearts and confetti.

## A practical visual grammar

The following are proposed production rules, not scientifically proven ratios.

| Layer | Rule | Why it helps this game | Failure to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Silhouette | One dominant body mass, tiny supporting limbs, one identifying appendage | Recognition at thumbnail size | Three recolors of the same blob |
| Face | Low, compact face; substantial forehead; dark eyes and restrained mouth | Affection plus mobile readability | Extreme eyes or a permanently manic smile |
| Shape rhythm | Soft large forms, a few smaller contrasting shapes | Keeps roundness from becoming monotonous | Spikes, hard armor, weapon-like attachments |
| Line | Warm brown outline, slight organic irregularity | Connects all characters to the illustrated room | Each asset having unrelated line weight |
| Color | Oatmeal and cream surfaces; sage actions; apricot as a small accent | Calm hierarchy and warmth | Low-contrast beige text on beige UI |
| Material | Gouache grain in artwork, clean surfaces behind functional text | Handmade character without reducing legibility | Texture under every label |
| Light | One understandable window source, soft reflected light | Makes the room feel inhabited | Neon highlights and competing glow effects |
| Composition | A clear floor/rug under the companion; detailed objects at the edges | Provides a resting place for the eye | Busy scenery directly behind the face |
| Scale | Pet feels small relative to a window and blanket, but large enough to read | A tiny inhabitant, not an app sticker | Mascot floating without contact with the scene |
| Motion | Short reaction with anticipation/settling; stillness between actions | Intent and perceived personality | All objects bobbing continuously |
| UI | One primary action; optional details disclosed progressively | Predictable, low-effort control | A dashboard of equally loud cards |
| Copy | Acknowledgment, invitation, explicit save state | Kindness without ambiguity | Guilt, nagging, or unclear consequences |

A cozy palette does not have to be pastel. What matters here is controlled saturation and a clear value hierarchy. We use light surfaces and dark green-brown text. Large artwork can hold richer color while controls stay quiet. A future evening mode should reduce brightness while preserving the same hierarchy, rather than placing a dark translucent wash over all text.

### Character direction

**Mugi / むぎ — the leaf collector.** A custard-colored seed body, asymmetric sage sprout, and one carefully held leaf. Its habit is showing you a leaf it thinks is especially good. The collectible suggests both a personality and a future game interaction.

**Sumi / すみ — the quiet idea keeper.** A blue-grey ink-drop silhouette, pale belly, and a small blunt pencil. It draws little circles while thinking. It offers a sketch rather than celebrating at full volume. Its narrower top makes it identifiable beside Mugi without changing the common facial grammar.

**Haku / はく — the expert at taking a break.** A cloud outline, sleepy face, peach scarf, and small soft feet. It notices warm places and makes room beside itself. It is a relaxed companion rather than a pet that needs rescuing from exhaustion.

In this UI pass these are freely selectable **appearances**, not three separate owned pets or earned evolutions. They share the user's existing progress. Future evolution should retain a recognizable facial identity and signature object; growth need not mean becoming taller, fiercer, armored, or less cute.

The atlas contains idle poses. The implemented greeting is a short transform animation, not a finished multi-frame character animation set. For a later animation production pass, create consistent turnarounds and six actions: look up, work on a tiny task, present a find, greet, eat, and sleep. Distinguish actions by pose and prop, not by floating status icons alone.

## UI changes made in the repository

- Replaced the active dashboard's layered console shell with a room-and-diary layout, quiet navigation, and a readable Japanese type stack.
- Replaced the neon pixel pet panel with a layered illustrated room and three original character appearances. Greeting a creature produces a personality-specific response and a brief reaction. The art is stored locally in `public/art/`.
- Reworked the timer into one obvious start/finish action. Client, label, and billing fields remain available in an optional disclosure. It still uses the existing work-session and XP behavior.
- Rebuilt the growth panel in the same paper/sage language. Attribute identity and costs remain compatible with existing saves. Buttons disable while an upgrade is pending.
- Reworked account creation/sign-in using the same visual language and Japanese labels. Authentication callbacks remain the existing Firebase flow.
- Added `/cozy-preview`, a clearly labeled, non-persistent local demo with appearance switching, greetings, time recording, a 25-minute fast-forward control, growth upgrades, and a session list. Without Firebase configuration, `/` opens that preview instead of attempting Firebase initialization.
- Added an optional companion appearance field with a default for existing saves. Palette updates use a nested field path so they do not erase the companion selection.

Existing business reports, invoice tools, planning, and detailed settings remain functional and retain their older card styling in authenticated secondary screens. This is a first complete pass through the main companion experience, not a claim that every legacy tool has been redesigned or translated. The earlier proposed outing/evolution economy is still a future gameplay change; the existing uncapped XP formula is unchanged.

## Accessibility is part of calmness

W3C's contrast guidance calls for at least 4.5:1 for normal-size text and 3:1 for large text, with defined exceptions. We use dark text on light surfaces and separate decorative artwork from functional labels. [Contrast guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html).

Non-essential interaction animation can be disabled, as described in W3C's animation guidance. Our short greeting respects `prefers-reduced-motion`; there is no default audio or perpetual pet movement. [Animation guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html).

Use visible keyboard focus, native buttons, explicit field labels, and 44px main controls. Announce greeting/save results, not every timer tick. Test small screens and long Japanese names. Keyboard usability and readable disabled states matter as much as whether a screenshot looks peaceful. These checks support accessibility but do not constitute a full WCAG conformance audit.

## What to validate with cozy-game players

Recruit a qualitative first group of 8–12 Japanese adults with varying work schedules and cozy-game tastes. Include people who enjoy decorating and narrative games as well as creature collectors; don't recruit only fans of one franchise. Include mobile and desktop use. This pilot will find usability and preference issues, not provide representative market estimates.

1. **Five-second recognition:** show the old interface and the room direction in counterbalanced order. Ask what the product is and how they expect it to feel before explaining it. Check whether the new screen communicates a companion game rather than merely a timer with decoration.
2. **Thumbnail test:** show characters at 48, 96, and 160 pixels. Ask participants to distinguish and later recognize them. Check silhouette as well as face. The leaf and pencil must survive reduction; fine texture need not.
3. **Attachment test:** offer all three with equal prominence. Ask who they would choose, what they think it enjoys, and why. Compare with the intended personalities without leading with “cute.”
4. **Interaction test:** begin a session, finish it, greet a companion, and change appearance. Measure task confusion separately from emotional response. Test a keyboard and reduced motion.
5. **Comfort test:** ask whether the art feels welcoming, childish, visually busy, or generic. Compare the current painterly room with a flatter, quieter room if detailed backgrounds distract from the companion.
6. **Repeated-use test:** after several working days, ask what they remember. A named creature, a gesture, or an object is a stronger attachment signal than merely remembering an XP total. Watch for the art becoming distracting or repetitive.
7. **Agency test:** let users leave mid-session and return after a missed day. Ask if they feel invited back or obligated. Future care mechanics should be evaluated here before release.

Keep “cute,” “cozy,” “charming,” and “usable” as separate ratings. A design can win one and lose another. Decide using observed task behavior and explanations as well as ratings; do not optimize for more hours worked. No conversion uplift, retention estimate, gender preference, or market-size claim is established by this research.

## Production follow-through

The next art milestone should be a small controlled animation set, not more static species. Lock silhouette, face placement, line weight, palette, and prop sizes on a model sheet; then render each action at its real UI size. Add room collectibles only once placing them is a real game action. Keep generated asset prompts and originals with the project so subsequent artwork can be reviewed against the same constraints.

Two useful variants to test next are **storybook room** (this implementation) and **soft pixel room** (same hierarchy and character identities, lower texture density). That isolates rendering style without conflating it with completely different navigation or gameplay. Keep the room diary as the common structure.
